In September 2026 TypeSafe AI released Jev, a model that does not write text [@typesafe]. You give it some context, a question and a list of allowed answers, and it returns a probability for each answer in a single forward pass. Within weeks, AutoTrust AI released open copies called JEV-9B and JEV-27B and reported a calibration error below 0.001 [@autotrust]. Several other teams followed with their own versions.

The selling point of all these models is the probability. A model that says "refund approved, 97%" is supposed to be right about 97% of the time when it says that. This post asks whether that holds on questions with known correct answers. Along the way it covers what calibration actually guarantees, why the most widely reported calibration metric is a poor one, and what to measure instead.

The short answer: pooled over every question we asked, the JEV models are almost perfectly calibrated. Split by dataset, they are underconfident on easy questions and overconfident on hard ones, and the two errors cancel in the pooled number. Every language-model-based decider we tested shows the same pattern. On the decision benchmarks the models' makers use, the same thing happens across kinds of question: a model can be overconfident on multiple choice and underconfident on yes/no at once. The full results are on the [benchmark leaderboard](/leaderboards/jev-calibration/) and the [decision benchmarks leaderboard](/leaderboards/decision-benchmarks/).

## Why the probability matters

Most uses of a decision model come down to a threshold. Act automatically when the model is at least 95% sure, and send everything else to a person. Approve the transaction above 0.9, block it below 0.2, review the rest. The threshold is chosen once and then trusted on every input.

That only works if 95% means the same thing everywhere. If the model's 95% is right 99% of the time on one kind of question and 80% of the time on another, a single threshold gives you very different error rates depending on what traffic arrives that day. The probability is the interface between the model and every system built on top of it. When it drifts, everything downstream drifts with it, silently.

## What JEV is

Jev is the first of what TypeSafe calls System One models: models that make a fast, typed decision instead of generating text [@typesafe]. A request names a question type, such as a choice between labelled options, a yes-or-no judgement, or a position on a scale, and the model fills in a probability for each allowed answer. There is no sampling and no parsing of free text, so the answer always has the requested shape.

JEV-9B and JEV-27B are AutoTrust's open reproductions [@autotrust]. Each freezes a Qwen language model, adds a small adapter and a dedicated output head, and trains only those new parts to match Jev's own answers on about half a million examples.<Sidenote>Training to match another model's probabilities is called distillation. The student learns the teacher's beliefs, including its mistakes and its uncertainty.</Sidenote> The other open models we test, Kev, JPT, Strands Decider, Open-Jev and Laya, were built by other teams on their own data. All of them speak the same request format, which makes a fair comparison possible.

## What calibration means

Take every question where the model's top answer came with a confidence of about 70%. If the model is calibrated, about 70% of those top answers are correct. The same must hold at every confidence level. Written out for the top answer $\hat y$ and its probability $\hat p$:

$$
\Pr\bigl(\hat y = y \mid \hat p = p\bigr) = p \quad \text{for every } p \in [0, 1]
$$

This is called top-label or confidence calibration [@guo2017]. A stronger version asks the whole probability vector to be right, including the probabilities on the answers the model did not pick. Top-label calibration is the version that matters for thresholds, and it is the one we measure.

A reliability diagram shows calibration directly. Group the questions by stated confidence, then plot each group's accuracy against its confidence. A calibrated model lies on the diagonal. Bars above the diagonal mean the model undersells itself, and bars below it mean the model oversells.

### Calibrated against what?

AutoTrust's figure of 0.001 compares JEV's probabilities with Jev's probabilities on Jev's own training topics [@autotrust]. That measures how faithfully the copy reproduces its teacher. It says nothing about whether either model's probabilities match how often their answers are right, because no correct answers are involved. A copy can match a miscalibrated teacher perfectly.

So in this study every number is computed against human gold labels from standard benchmarks, where the right answer is known.

## Calibration is not enough

Calibration is easy to satisfy in a useless way. Suppose a model answers questions correctly 77% of the time. Replace every confidence it reports with 77%. The new model makes exactly the same choices, and it is perfectly calibrated: among questions where it says 77%, which is all of them, it is right 77% of the time. It is also useless for deciding which answers to trust, because it gives every answer the same score.

What the constant model lacks is called refinement, sharpness or resolution [@degroot1983]: the ability to give high confidence to answers that turn out right and low confidence to answers that turn out wrong. A good probability needs both properties. Calibration makes the numbers honest. Resolution makes them informative.

There is a second gap. Calibration is an average over everything the model sees. A model can be calibrated over all its traffic while being badly overconfident on one slice and underconfident on another, as long as the errors cancel. This is exactly what we find with JEV. The fix in theory is to demand calibration on many slices at once, an idea formalised as multicalibration [@hebert2018]. In practice it means you should always check calibration on the slices you care about, and distrust any single pooled number.

## The trouble with ECE

The most widely reported calibration metric is the expected calibration error, ECE. Sort the questions into bins by confidence, take the gap between each bin's average confidence and its accuracy, and average the gaps, weighted by bin size [@guo2017]:

$$
\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{|B_b|}{n}\,\bigl|\,\mathrm{acc}(B_b) - \mathrm{conf}(B_b)\,\bigr|
$$

ECE is intuitive, since it is the average distance from the diagonal of a reliability diagram. It also has serious problems.

**It depends on choices that have nothing to do with the model.** The number of bins, and whether bins have equal width or equal numbers of questions, change the answer [@nixon2019]. With few questions per bin the estimate is noisy, and with many questions per bin it hides detail.

**It is biased upward, especially on small datasets.** Even a perfectly calibrated model shows a positive ECE on a finite sample, because each bin's accuracy is estimated from a handful of questions [@kumar2019; @roelofs2022]. We estimate this noise floor for every result by simulating a perfectly calibrated model with the same confidences and the same number of questions.<Sidenote>For each question we draw a correct or wrong outcome with probability equal to the model's confidence, compute ECE on the simulated outcomes, and average over many draws.</Sidenote> On GPQA, with 198 questions, the floor is 0.079, half of JEV-9B's measured ECE of 0.157. Some of what looks like miscalibration is just a small sample.

**It can be gamed, and it cannot tell useful models from useless ones.** The constant model from the previous section has an ECE of zero. So does any model that hedges every answer to its average accuracy. ECE rewards honesty and ignores informativeness, which means a lower ECE does not mean a better model.

**It jumps.** Nudge one prediction across a bin boundary and ECE can change abruptly, so it is not a smooth function of the model's outputs. This makes it a shaky basis for comparing models and an awkward thing to optimise [@blasiok2023].

There are better estimators. A debiased version subtracts the expected sampling error [@kumar2019]. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov calibration error avoids bins by comparing cumulative curves [@gupta2021]. Smooth ECE replaces bins with a kernel and comes with a principled reliability diagram [@smoothece]. We report several of these on the leaderboard and they mostly agree on rankings. None of them fixes the deeper problem, which is that any calibration metric alone ignores resolution. For comparing models you want a score that rewards both.

## The Brier score, and why proper matters

The Brier score is the oldest answer to that problem [@brier1950]. For the top-label version, it is the squared gap between the model's confidence and the outcome:

$$
\mathrm{Brier} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(\hat p_i - \mathbb{1}[\hat y_i = y_i]\bigr)^2
$$

A confident right answer scores near 0, and a confident wrong answer scores near 1. It needs no bins and has no tuning knobs.

Its key property is that it is a strictly proper scoring rule [@gneiting2007]. A scoring rule is proper if a forecaster minimises their expected score by reporting what they actually believe. You can see this for Brier in one line. If the true chance of being right is $p$ and the model reports $q$, the expected score is

$$
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(q - Y)^2\bigr] = (q - p)^2 + p(1 - p)
$$

which is smallest exactly when $q = p$. No amount of hedging or exaggeration helps. ECE has no such property, which is why the constant model can game it.

The Brier score also splits cleanly into the two properties from earlier [@murphy1973]:

$$
\mathrm{Brier} = \underbrace{\mathrm{reliability}}_{\text{miscalibration}} - \underbrace{\mathrm{resolution}}_{\text{informativeness}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{uncertainty}}_{\text{difficulty}}
$$

Reliability is the calibration error, measured as a squared gap. Resolution measures how much the model's confidence separates right answers from wrong ones. Uncertainty depends only on accuracy, and it is the score of the constant model. Here is the split for JEV-9B and its untrained base model, pooled over all 21,150 questions:

| | Accuracy | Reliability | Resolution | Uncertainty | Brier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen3.5-9B, before training | 74.0% | 0.0136 | 0.0446 | 0.1926 | 0.161 |
| JEV-9B | 77.4% | 0.0001 | 0.0445 | 0.1751 | 0.131 |
| JEV-9B's answers, constant 77.4% confidence | 77.4% | 0 | 0 | 0.1751 | 0.175 |

Three things stand out. Training removed almost all the pooled miscalibration. It left resolution unchanged: the trained model separates its right answers from its wrong ones no better than the base model did. And the Brier improvement comes from those two sources plus higher accuracy, which lowers the uncertainty term. The constant model in the last row has perfect calibration and the worst Brier score of the three, which is the correct ranking.<Sidenote>These terms are estimated from the same confidence bins as the reliability diagrams, so they carry a small binning error. The sum matches the directly computed Brier score to within 0.001.</Sidenote>

## What the Brier score misses

The Brier score is the right default for comparing models, but it has its own blind spots.

**It mixes difficulty with quality.** The uncertainty term depends only on how hard the questions are. A Brier score of 0.22 on GPQA and 0.04 on ARC mostly reflects that GPQA is hard. Brier scores are comparable between models on the same questions and meaningless across datasets.

**It forgives confident mistakes.** The penalty is bounded at 1. A wrong answer given at 99% costs 0.98, barely more than one given at 90%, which costs 0.81. Log loss charges 4.6 and 2.3 for the same two mistakes, and it grows without limit as confidence approaches certainty. If one confidently wrong decision is catastrophic for you, log loss or a cost-weighted metric fits better. We report log loss on the leaderboard too.

**The top-label version scores only the chosen answer.** It is proper for the event "the top answer is right" and says nothing about how the model spreads its remaining probability over the other options. When the full distribution matters, for instance to build prediction sets, use the multiclass Brier score, which sums over every option and runs from 0 to 2. Our data files include it.

**It averages over every possible threshold.** The Brier score equals the expected cost of thresholded decisions when every threshold is equally likely [@hernandez2012]. Real deployments use one threshold, or a narrow range. A model can have the better Brier score and still be worse at the 95% threshold you actually use. If you know your operating point, measure error rates there.

## What we measured

We asked eight models the same questions from seven datasets: MMLU, ARC-Challenge, CommonsenseQA, OpenBookQA, TruthfulQA, GPQA Diamond, and Under Pressure, a benchmark of intent, emotion and topic questions with 2 to 16 options. Each model was asked through the server its authors ship, with lettered options, the format Jev's documentation uses. Every model saw the same 21,150 questions, apart from one GPQA question missing for JEV-27B, and every score is computed against the benchmark's gold answer. Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals from resampling questions.

## Underconfident on easy questions, overconfident on hard ones

Pooled over all 21,150 questions, JEV-9B's ECE is 0.010, close to perfect. Computed per dataset and then averaged, it is 0.064, six times higher. The gap between those two numbers is the whole story.

On easy benchmarks the model is right more often than it claims. On ARC it answers 94% of questions correctly but reports less confidence than that. On hard benchmarks it claims more than it delivers. On GPQA, a set of graduate-level science questions, it is right 36% of the time and overstates its confidence by about 12 points on average.

Psychologists know this pattern from people. Lichtenstein and Fischhoff called it the hard–easy effect: we are overconfident on hard questions and underconfident on easy ones [@lichtenstein1977]. One plausible reason it appears in models too: a confidence score may track how familiar a question looks more than how likely this particular answer is to be right, so it moves less than accuracy does as difficulty changes.

Comparing JEV-9B with its own base model shows where the pattern comes from. The untrained Qwen model is underconfident on six of the seven datasets, all except GPQA. Training on Jev's answers raises its confidence on every dataset by a similar amount, between 0.05 and 0.16. That lift is exactly right for middling datasets, too small for the easiest ones, and too much for the hardest.

## Why one temperature cannot fix it

The standard repair for a miscalibrated model is temperature scaling: divide the model's scores by one number before turning them into probabilities, choosing the number on held-out data [@guo2017]. A temperature above 1 softens overconfident predictions, and one below 1 sharpens underconfident ones.

For each dataset we found the temperature that would make JEV-9B best calibrated against the gold labels. It ranges from 0.67 on ARC and OpenBookQA to 1.98 on GPQA. No single number serves both ends. A temperature tuned on a mix of questions repairs the average and leaves the hard–easy pattern in place, and one tuned on one dataset makes others worse. Temperature scaling is still worth doing, but only on data that looks like your own traffic.

## Eight models, one pattern

The other teams' models were trained on their own data, without Jev's answers. Kev, JPT, Strands Decider and Open-Jev all show the same shape: cautious on easy benchmarks and too sure on hard ones. So the pattern is not something the JEV copies inherited from Jev. It seems to come with reading a probability off a language model in a single pass. The one exception is Laya, a small encoder model rather than a language model, which is overconfident on every dataset.

## On decision tasks, by kind of question

The benchmarks above are standard multiple-choice tests, and decision models are built for something narrower: routing a ticket, checking a rule, rating a review. So we also ran every model on the decision benchmarks AutoTrust and NeoHorse report for their own models. These are Kev's three decision suites, VitaminC and MASSIVE, and the original and easy tiers of JevBench, about 7,200 questions per model, all with answers from people or from rules.<Sidenote>Two other benchmarks in their reports, JevBench's hard tier and Nimble, have answers written by language models. We ran them but leave them out of every summary.</Sidenote> Our runs reproduce the published accuracy scores for Kev-4B and Laya to within half a point; JEV-27B comes out 1.8 points below AutoTrust's figure.

These benchmarks mix three kinds of question: multiple choice, yes/no, and ratings on an ordered scale. On them, JEV-27B has the best Brier score, 0.099. JEV-9B and JPT-4B follow at 0.101 and 0.102, too close to separate.

The kinds of question pull calibration in different directions. JEV-27B is overconfident on multiple choice, by 0.058 on average, and underconfident on yes/no, by 0.072. Averaged over everything, the two partly cancel. Strands Decider is close to calibrated on multiple choice and badly underconfident on ratings, by 0.245. A threshold tuned on one kind of question will not carry over to another.

Training on Jev's answers shows the same uniform lift as before. The untrained Qwen model is underconfident overall, by 0.095. JEV-9B is slightly overconfident, by 0.037. On yes/no questions the base model was already calibrated, and JEV-9B stays close, slightly underconfident at 0.021, because its accuracy rose more than its confidence.

The benchmark matters as much as the kind of question. On VitaminC, which asks whether evidence supports a claim, six of the eight models are overconfident, among them JEV-27B by 0.121 and Kev-4B by 0.156. On MASSIVE, which sorts assistant requests into scenarios, Kev-4B and Open-Jev are strongly underconfident while the JEV models are overconfident. Kev-4B is close to perfect on Kev's own suites, partly because it was trained on that data.

## What to do about it

If you deploy one of these models behind a confidence threshold, the threshold will behave differently on easy and hard inputs. Three things help.

**Calibrate on your own data.** A few hundred labelled examples from your real traffic are enough to fit a temperature or check a reliability diagram. Do it per slice if your traffic mixes easy and hard cases.

**Report proper scores.** Use the Brier score or log loss to compare models, ECE only as a diagnostic, and always look at the reliability diagram on the slices that matter.

**Use conformal prediction when you need a guarantee.** Conformal prediction turns a model's scores into a set of answers that contains the right one with a chosen probability, such as 90%, using a small labelled calibration set [@angelopoulos2021]. Its guarantee holds even when the probabilities are miscalibrated. It does depend on the calibration data looking like the test data, the same assumption temperature scaling needs. Here training clearly helps: at 90% coverage, JEV-9B's prediction sets average 1.75 options against 2.04 for its base model, and JEV-27B's average 1.43. Better resolution shows up as smaller sets.

## Limitations

On the multiple-choice benchmarks, each model was asked in one format, lettered options with the option text attached. A model tuned for a different format may do somewhat better in its authors' own tests. On the decision benchmarks, JEV's rating slots cannot show what each level means, so we asked rating questions as multiple choice over the level descriptions; that was more accurate than the alternative we tried. JPT-4B's server could not read some inputs and skipped about 2% of the decision-benchmark questions. One very long GPQA question is missing from JEV-27B's results because it did not fit in memory. Finally, we have not yet run TypeSafe's own Jev on these questions. That comparison would show whether the hard–easy pattern lives in the teacher too, and it is next.

## Run it yourself

Every number in this post comes from the [repository](https://github.com/harit7/jev-croq), and the data behind every chart can be downloaded from the [leaderboard](/leaderboards/jev-calibration/). This scores JEV-9B on two of the benchmarks:

```bash
# answers are cached, so a rerun only scores new questions
python -m jevcroq decide-mcqa --model-dir models/JEV-9B \
    --datasets arc_challenge gpqa_diamond \
    --cache outputs/jev-9b/cache.jsonl --out outputs/jev-9b/mcqa.jsonl
```
