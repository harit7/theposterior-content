Imagine you run customer support for an online shop. A model reads each refund request and replies with a verdict and a number: *approve, 97%*. You set a simple rule. Anything above 95% goes through automatically, and everything else goes to a person.

That rule quietly assumes something: when the model says 97%, it's right about 97 times out of 100. If it's really right 80 times out of 100 on some kinds of request, your automatic lane is approving plenty of mistakes, and nothing in your logs will tell you.

A new family of models makes exactly this promise. So we put the promise to the test.

## A model that only answers questions

In September 2026, TypeSafe AI released Jev [@typesafe]. It doesn't chat or write essays. You hand it some context, a question and a list of allowed answers, and it hands back a probability for each answer. One pass through the network, a few hundred milliseconds, done.

Within weeks AutoTrust AI released open copies, JEV-9B and JEV-27B. They were built by teaching a Qwen language model to imitate Jev's answers on about half a million examples [@autotrust].<Sidenote>This is called distillation. The student learns to copy the teacher's probabilities, including its mistakes and its doubts.</Sidenote> Other teams followed with their own models, trained on their own data: Kev, JPT, Strands Decider, Open-Jev and Laya. They all speak the same request format, so we can ask every one of them the same questions.

AutoTrust's model card says JEV's calibration error is below 0.001. That's a remarkable number. Let's see what it means.

## What "calibrated" promises

Think of a weather forecaster. Collect every day she said "70% chance of rain". If it rained on about 70% of those days, and the same holds for her 20% days and her 90% days, she's calibrated. Her numbers mean what they say.

A decision model works the same way. Take every answer it gave with about 70% confidence. If about 70% of those answers were right, and the same holds at every other confidence level, the model is calibrated [@guo2017]. In symbols, for the top answer $\hat y$ and its confidence $\hat p$:

$$
\Pr\bigl(\hat y = y \mid \hat p = p\bigr) = p \quad \text{for every } p \in [0, 1]
$$

Here's the catch with AutoTrust's 0.001. It compares JEV's probabilities with Jev's probabilities, on Jev's own training topics. It measures how faithfully the copy imitates its teacher. No correct answers appear anywhere in that measurement, so a perfect copy of a badly calibrated teacher would score just as well.

So we checked against the truth. We asked all eight models the same 21,150 questions from seven benchmarks with known answers: MMLU, ARC-Challenge, CommonsenseQA, OpenBookQA, TruthfulQA, GPQA Diamond, and Under Pressure, a set of intent, emotion and topic questions with 2 to 16 options.<Sidenote>Each model was asked through the server its authors ship, with lettered options, the format Jev's documentation uses. One very long GPQA question is missing from JEV-27B's results.</Sidenote>

## First look: nearly perfect

Throw all 21,150 questions into one pile and JEV-9B looks superb. Its calibration error is 0.010. When it says 80%, it's right very close to 80% of the time.

Then split the pile by benchmark.

## Second look: it depends on the question

On easy benchmarks, JEV-9B is right more often than it claims. It answers 94% of ARC questions correctly and still sounds less sure than that. On hard benchmarks it's the other way round. On GPQA, a set of graduate-level science questions, it's right 36% of the time and overstates its confidence by about 12 points.

Measured benchmark by benchmark and then averaged, JEV-9B's calibration error is 0.064, six times the pooled figure. The two mistakes cancel when you pool everything, and that's why the pooled number looks so good.

People do this too. Psychologists call it the hard–easy effect: we're overconfident on hard questions and underconfident on easy ones [@lichtenstein1977]. One plausible reason models share it is that confidence tracks how familiar a question looks more than whether this particular answer is right. So it moves less than accuracy does as the questions get harder.

## Where the pattern comes from

We can watch it form. JEV-9B is built on Qwen3.5-9B, so we asked the untrained Qwen model the same questions. Before training, it's underconfident on six of the seven benchmarks. Training on Jev's answers raises its confidence on all of them by a similar amount, between 0.05 and 0.16.

That one-size lift is just right for the middling benchmarks, too small for the easiest and too big for the hardest.

The usual repair is temperature scaling: divide the model's scores by one number, which softens or sharpens every probability at once [@guo2017]. We found the best temperature for each benchmark. It runs from 0.67 on ARC and OpenBookQA to 1.98 on GPQA. No single number can serve both ends. Tune one on your own traffic and you'll fix your average, while the split between easy and hard questions stays.

## It isn't just JEV

The other teams never saw Jev's answers. Kev, JPT, Strands Decider and Open-Jev show the same shape anyway: cautious on easy benchmarks, too sure on hard ones. So JEV didn't inherit this from Jev. It seems to come with reading a probability off a language model in one pass. The odd one out is Laya, a small encoder model, which is overconfident on every benchmark.

## How do you score a probability?

### The 77% trick

Here's a fun way to cheat. Take JEV-9B, which is right 77.4% of the time across our questions. Keep every answer it picks, and replace every confidence it reports with 77.4%. This new model is perfectly calibrated: it always says 77.4%, and it's right 77.4% of the time.

It's also useless. It can't tell you which answers to trust, because every answer gets the same number. Calibration makes a model's numbers honest. A good model also needs them to be informative, high for answers that turn out right and low for answers that turn out wrong. Forecasters call that resolution [@degroot1983].

### Why ECE falls for it

The most widely reported calibration metric is the expected calibration error, ECE. Sort answers into bins by confidence, measure the gap between confidence and accuracy in each bin, and average the gaps [@guo2017]:

$$
\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{|B_b|}{n}\,\bigl|\,\mathrm{acc}(B_b) - \mathrm{conf}(B_b)\,\bigr|
$$

The 77% trick scores a perfect zero. ECE only checks honesty, so it can't tell a useful model from a useless one. It has a few other quirks too:

- **The score depends on how you draw the bins.** Change their number or their widths and it moves [@nixon2019].
- **Small samples look miscalibrated.** Even a perfectly calibrated model shows some ECE on a finite set of questions [@kumar2019; @roelofs2022]. On GPQA's 198 questions, that noise floor is 0.079, half of JEV-9B's measured 0.157.<Sidenote>We estimate the floor by simulating a perfectly calibrated model with the same confidences: each answer comes out right with probability equal to its confidence.</Sidenote>
- **It jumps.** Nudge one confidence across a bin edge and the score changes abruptly [@blasiok2023].

On big benchmarks the three agree and the floor is tiny. On small ones they spread out and the floor grows. Better versions exist: a debiased ECE [@kumar2019], a bin-free version based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test [@gupta2021], and smooth ECE [@smoothece]. They mostly agree with each other, and none of them catches the 77% trick.

### The Brier score sees through it

The Brier score comes from 1950s weather forecasting [@brier1950]. For each answer, take the model's confidence, subtract 1 if the answer was right and 0 if it was wrong, square the result, and average:

$$
\mathrm{Brier} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(\hat p_i - \mathbb{1}[\hat y_i = y_i]\bigr)^2
$$

Say 95% and be right, and you pay 0.0025. Say 95% and be wrong, and you pay 0.9025. That asymmetry is the whole idea.

The Brier score is a proper scoring rule [@gneiting2007]: your best strategy is to report what you actually believe. One line shows why. If the real chance of being right is $p$ and you report $q$, your expected score is

$$
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(q - Y)^2\bigr] = (q - p)^2 + p(1 - p)
$$

The first term disappears only when you tell the truth, $q = p$. The second is the price of the question being hard, and no amount of spin changes it.

Better still, the Brier score splits into the pieces we care about [@murphy1973]:

$$
\mathrm{Brier} = \underbrace{\mathrm{reliability}}_{\text{miscalibration}} - \underbrace{\mathrm{resolution}}_{\text{informativeness}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{uncertainty}}_{\text{difficulty}}
$$

Here's that split for JEV-9B, its untrained base model, and the 77% trick, over all 21,150 questions:

| | Accuracy | Reliability | Resolution | Uncertainty | Brier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen3.5-9B, before training | 74.0% | 0.0136 | 0.0446 | 0.1926 | 0.161 |
| JEV-9B | 77.4% | 0.0001 | 0.0445 | 0.1751 | 0.131 |
| The 77% trick | 77.4% | 0 | 0 | 0.1751 | 0.175 |

Read it row by row. Training wiped out nearly all the pooled miscalibration. It left resolution exactly where it was: the trained model separates right answers from wrong ones no better than the base model did. And the 77% trick, perfectly calibrated, gets the worst Brier score of the three, exactly as it should.<Sidenote>These terms come from the same confidence bins as the reliability diagrams, so they carry a small binning error. Their sum matches the directly computed Brier score to within 0.001.</Sidenote>

### Where the Brier score falls short

It's the right default, with four blind spots worth knowing:

- **Hard questions inflate it.** GPQA's 0.22 next to ARC's 0.04 mostly says GPQA is hard. Only compare models on the same questions.
- **It forgives confident mistakes.** Being wrong at 99% costs 0.98, barely more than being wrong at 90%, which costs 0.81. Log loss charges 4.6 and 2.3. If one confident mistake can sink you, use log loss.
- **The usual version ignores the runner-up answers.** If you care about the whole distribution, use the multiclass Brier score, which sums over every option.
- **It averages over every possible threshold** [@hernandez2012]. You probably use one. If you know yours, measure error rates there too.

## Back to the scoreboard

Ranked by Brier score, JEV-27B comes first. JPT-4B and JEV-9B tie for second and third, because their intervals overlap, and JPT-4B is less than half JEV-9B's size. The untrained Qwen model and Strands Decider tie for fifth and sixth.

Rank by ECE instead and two pairs swap places. JEV-9B edges past JPT-4B, and Strands Decider jumps ahead of the untrained Qwen model. Strands Decider's confidences sit closer to its accuracy, but its accuracy is much lower, and the Brier score counts both. Read only the ECE column and you'd pick the worse model.

## The jobs these models were built for

Exam questions are a tough test, and decision models are built for narrower jobs: routing a ticket, checking a rule, rating a review. So we also ran every model on the decision benchmarks AutoTrust and NeoHorse use for their own models. That's Kev's three decision suites, VitaminC, MASSIVE and JevBench, about 7,200 questions per model, all with answers from people or from rules.<Sidenote>Two more benchmarks in their reports, JevBench's hard tier and Nimble, have answers written by language models. We ran them and kept them out of every summary.</Sidenote> As a sanity check, our accuracy scores for Kev-4B and Laya land within half a point of the published ones. JEV-27B comes out 1.8 points lower.

These benchmarks mix three kinds of question: multiple choice, yes/no, and ratings on a scale. JEV-27B has the best Brier score, 0.099, with JEV-9B and JPT-4B right behind at 0.101 and 0.102.

And the cancelling act shows up again, this time across kinds of question. JEV-27B is overconfident on multiple choice, by 0.058, and underconfident on yes/no, by 0.072. Strands Decider is nearly calibrated on multiple choice and badly underconfident on ratings, by 0.245. A threshold tuned on one kind of question won't carry over to another.

The benchmark matters too. On VitaminC, which asks whether evidence supports a claim, six of the eight models are overconfident, JEV-27B by 0.121 and Kev-4B by 0.156. On MASSIVE, which sorts assistant requests into topics, Kev-4B and Open-Jev undersell themselves while the JEV models oversell. Kev-4B is close to perfect on Kev's own suites, which it was partly trained on. The [decision benchmarks leaderboard](/leaderboards/decision-benchmarks/) breaks all of this down by kind of question.

## So what should you do?

Back to the refund queue. Your 95% threshold will let through more mistakes on hard requests than on easy ones. Three habits help.

**Check calibration on your own traffic.** A few hundred labelled examples are enough to draw a reliability diagram or fit a temperature. If your traffic mixes easy and hard cases, check each kind separately.

**Pick models with a proper score.** Compare models with the Brier score or log loss. Treat ECE as a diagnostic, and always look at the reliability diagram.

**Use conformal prediction when you need a guarantee.** Conformal prediction turns scores into a set of answers that contains the right one with a probability you choose, such as 90% [@angelopoulos2021]. The guarantee holds even when the probabilities are miscalibrated, as long as your labelled examples look like your real traffic. Training helps here: at 90% coverage, JEV-9B's sets average 1.75 options against 2.04 for its base model, and JEV-27B's average 1.43.

## The fine print

- **One format per model.** On the exam benchmarks every model got lettered options. A model tuned to another format may do a little better in its authors' own tests.
- **Ratings, asked as multiple choice.** JEV's rating slots can't show what each level means, so we asked rating questions as multiple choice over the level descriptions. That was more accurate than the alternative we tried.
- **A few skipped questions.** JPT-4B's server couldn't read some inputs and skipped about 2% of the decision-benchmark questions.
- **The teacher is still untested.** We haven't yet run TypeSafe's own Jev on these questions. That would show whether the hard–easy pattern lives in the teacher too, and it's next.

## Try it yourself

Every number here comes from the [repository](https://github.com/harit7/jev-croq), and the data behind every chart can be downloaded from the leaderboards. This scores JEV-9B on two of the benchmarks:

```bash
# answers are cached, so a rerun only scores new questions
python -m jevcroq decide-mcqa --model-dir models/JEV-9B \
    --datasets arc_challenge gpqa_diamond \
    --cache outputs/jev-9b/cache.jsonl --out outputs/jev-9b/mcqa.jsonl
```
